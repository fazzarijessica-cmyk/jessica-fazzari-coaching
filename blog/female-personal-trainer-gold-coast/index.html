Why some people prefer a female personal trainer

Plenty of people are happy training with any good coach. Others actively search for a female personal trainer, and the reasons are usually practical and personal rather than anything to do with ability. Common ones include:

  • Comfort. Some people simply feel more at ease being coached by a woman, especially when they're new to the gym or talking about things like body composition, energy or how training fits around the rest of life.
  • Communication. Feeling able to speak openly with your coach matters. If you'll ask more questions and be more honest about how training is going with a female coach, that's a good reason to choose one.
  • Hands-on cueing. In-person coaching sometimes involves a light touch to correct position. Some clients prefer that from a coach of the same gender.
  • Shared experience in the gym. For some, it's reassuring to train with someone who has walked into a weights area as a woman and knows what that first visit can feel like.

None of these are rules. They're just reasons people give, and they're all valid. What matters most is that you feel comfortable enough to turn up consistently.

What matters more than gender

Whoever you choose, the basics of a good trainer don't change. The Better Health Channel recommends checking:

  • qualifications, including first aid
  • experience and references
  • public liability and professional indemnity insurance

In Australia, the Certificate IV in Fitness is the standard qualification for personal trainers, and the Certificate III covers gym instruction. Registration bodies such as AUSactive also look for current first aid and CPR. Beyond the paperwork, look for a coach who has helped people with goals like yours, explains what's included in the price, and communicates in a way that suits you. There's a full checklist in how to choose a personal trainer.

Strength training: the basics, without the myths

Many people looking for a female PT want to get stronger, and some are wary of lifting weights. A few points worth knowing:

  • Strength training is recommended for everyone. Australia's physical activity guidelines advise adults aged 18–64 to do muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days a week, alongside 150–300 minutes of moderate activity (or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity) each week.
  • Building muscle is slow and deliberate. Worrying about getting “bulky” by accident is common, but adding significant muscle takes consistent training, enough food and a long time. A good program progresses at the pace you choose.
  • Progress shows up in more than one way. Lifting more, moving better and having more energy often arrive before visible changes, so it helps to track strength and how you feel, not just the scales.

Pregnancy, postpartum and health conditions

If you're pregnant, recently had a baby, or managing a health condition or injury, speak with your GP or physio before starting or changing a training program. They can tell you what's appropriate for your situation. Share that advice with your trainer so your program can work within it.

Pregnancy, postpartum recovery and medical conditions all need individual advice. Check with your GP or physio first, and tell your trainer about any guidance you've been given.

Female personal training on the Gold Coast: in person or online

The Gold Coast is a long, spread-out city, so where your trainer works matters. Jess coaches in person at World Gym Ashmore on Olsen Avenue, which is central. Ashmore borders Southport and is a short drive from Surfers Paradise, so it suits people across the middle of the coast. If you're further north or south, see our guides for Southport and online personal training on the Gold Coast.

Online coaching is the other option. You follow a personalised program in the Everfit app at any gym (or at home, if you have suitable equipment), and check in with your coach remotely. It's usually cheaper and more flexible, and it removes the pressure of being watched in a busy gym, which some people find helpful early on.

Whichever you choose, your first session or check-in should feel like a conversation, not a test. Expect to talk about your goals, training history and any injuries, and to start at an intensity that matches where you are now (Nuffield Health). If a trainer pushes you to exhaustion on day one without asking those questions, that tells you something about how they coach.
