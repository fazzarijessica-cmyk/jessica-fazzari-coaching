Can personal trainers give nutrition advice or meal plans in Australia?

Jessica Fazzari Coaching · 7 November 2026 · 5 min read

Yes, but only general advice. In Australia, qualified personal trainers can give general healthy-eating information in line with the Australian Dietary Guidelines. Individualised diet plans, specific therapeutic diets and supplement prescriptions are outside a PT's scope – those belong with an Accredited Practising Dietitian or your GP.

What the Cert IV in Fitness says about nutrition

The standard qualification for personal trainers in Australia is the Certificate IV in Fitness. One of its units, SISFFIT026 (training.gov.au), covers providing healthy-eating information. It trains PTs to share general nutrition advice consistent with the Australian Dietary Guidelines.

The same unit is clear about what it does not cover. Developing individualised diet plans, recommending specific diets and advising on supplements are excluded. When a client has a medical condition that affects what they should eat, the expectation is that the trainer refers them on to a dietitian.

For more on what the Cert IV involves, see what qualifications a personal trainer needs in Australia.

What a PT can help you with

General nutrition support from a trainer can still be very useful. Within their scope, a personal trainer can typically:

  • Explain the basics of the Australian Dietary Guidelines – vegetables, fruit, whole grains, protein foods and dairy or alternatives.
  • Talk through general principles like regular meals, hydration and eating enough to fuel training.
  • Share general education on protein, carbohydrates and fats and the role each plays.
  • Offer recipe ideas and meal inspiration.
  • Help you build habits, such as planning your shopping, prepping lunches or cutting back on takeaway.
  • Keep you accountable to the changes you've chosen to make.

For most healthy adults who simply want to eat a bit better and support their training, this kind of guidance – combined with consistent exercise – goes a long way.

What a PT shouldn't do

Some things sit outside a personal trainer's scope of practice. A PT shouldn't:

  • Write a fully individualised meal plan telling you exactly what to eat at each meal.
  • Put you on a specific diet (for example a medically focused or highly restrictive one).
  • Prescribe or recommend supplements.
  • Give dietary treatment for medical conditions such as diabetes, coeliac disease, kidney disease, food allergies, gut conditions or eating disorders.

If a trainer is offering these, it's worth asking about their qualifications. Some PTs hold additional nutrition or dietetics qualifications, but the Cert IV on its own does not cover this work.

If you have a medical condition, are pregnant, have a history of disordered eating or take medication that affects your diet, speak with your GP or an Accredited Practising Dietitian before making significant changes to how you eat.

Meal plans vs meal guidance: what's the difference?

The terms get used loosely, so it helps to separate them.

Many people don't actually need a rigid plan. Learning how to put together balanced meals you enjoy is often easier to stick with than following a set menu.

When to see a dietitian instead

A dietitian is the right person when:

  • You have a diagnosed condition where food is part of the treatment.
  • You have specific allergies or intolerances to manage.
  • You've had a difficult relationship with food or a history of disordered eating.
  • You want a detailed, individualised eating plan.
  • You're considering supplements and want advice on whether you need them.

Your trainer and dietitian can work alongside each other. The dietitian handles your eating plan; the trainer handles your training and helps you stay consistent. A good PT will happily refer you on when something is outside their lane – it's part of the job, as covered in what a personal trainer actually does.

Does nutrition matter for training results?

Yes. Whatever your goal – getting stronger, building muscle, improving fitness or changing body composition – what you eat affects your energy, recovery and progress. Training and nutrition work together, which is why general nutrition education is part of many coaching programs.

If weight loss is your main goal, the relationship between exercise and eating is covered in can a personal trainer help you lose weight.

Questions to ask a trainer about nutrition support

If nutrition help is part of the reason you're choosing a trainer, ask a few simple questions before you start:

  • What kind of nutrition support is included – education, recipe ideas, habit coaching or something else?
  • Is it general guidance, or are you being offered an individualised plan? If it's a plan, what qualifications sit behind it?
  • How will nutrition be checked in on – weekly, fortnightly or only when you ask?
  • When would they refer you to a dietitian?

Clear answers tell you the trainer understands their scope and will look after you properly.

Quick answers

Can a personal trainer write me a meal plan?

Under the Cert IV in Fitness, individualised diet plans are outside a PT's scope. A trainer can give general healthy-eating advice, recipe ideas and habit support, but a tailored meal plan should come from an Accredited Practising Dietitian.

Can a personal trainer recommend supplements?

Supplement advice is excluded from the Cert IV nutrition unit. If you're thinking about supplements, talk to your GP or a dietitian.

What's the difference between a nutritionist and a dietitian?

In Australia, Accredited Practising Dietitians are university-trained and can provide individualised dietary treatment, including for medical conditions. If you need tailored advice, a dietitian is the safest choice.

Can a PT give me macros?

Many trainers offer general macro guidance as education to support training. If you have a medical condition or need a detailed individual plan, see a dietitian.
