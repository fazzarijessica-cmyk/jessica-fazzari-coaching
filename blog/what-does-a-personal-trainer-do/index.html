What does a personal trainer actually do?

Jessica Fazzari Coaching · 7 November 2026 · 5 min read

A personal trainer assesses where you're starting from, helps you set realistic goals, writes a training program and progresses it over time, coaches your technique, and keeps you accountable. They can also share general lifestyle and nutrition education, and refer you to other health professionals when something is outside their scope.

1. Assessment and goal setting

Good training starts with understanding you. In the first session or two, a trainer will usually:

  • Ask about your goals – what you want to achieve and why it matters to you.
  • Talk through your training history, lifestyle, schedule and any injuries or health conditions.
  • Have you complete a health screening form.
  • Run a simple movement assessment to see how you squat, hinge, push, pull and balance.
  • Take optional measurements or baseline numbers, if you want them.

From there, they help turn a general aim like "get fitter" into something more specific and measurable – for example, being able to do a set number of push-ups, lifting a certain weight, or training consistently three times a week for the next few months.

The first appointment is covered in detail in what to expect at your first personal training session.

2. Programming and progression

Programming is one of the most valuable things a trainer does – and the part most people underestimate. A program decides:

  • Which exercises you do, and in what order.
  • How many sets and reps, and how heavy.
  • How much rest you take.
  • How many days a week you train and what each day focuses on.
  • How all of this changes week to week.

The key idea is progression. To keep improving, your body needs a gradually increasing challenge – more weight, more reps, better technique or harder variations. A trainer plans that progression so you keep moving forward without doing too much too soon. They also adjust the plan when life gets in the way, when something feels off, or when you're progressing faster than expected.

3. Technique coaching

In person, a trainer watches every set and gives you cues in real time: where to put your feet, how to brace, how deep to go, what to feel and where. Online, this usually happens through video – you film your lifts and get written or recorded feedback.

Good technique matters because it helps you:

  • Train the muscles you're actually trying to train.
  • Lift heavier safely over time.
  • Reduce the risk of avoidable aches and injuries.
  • Feel confident doing the exercises on your own.

4. Accountability and motivation

Knowing what to do and actually doing it are two different things. A big part of a trainer's job is helping you stay consistent:

  • Booked sessions you're less likely to skip.
  • Check-ins on how training, sleep, energy and stress are going.
  • Tracking progress so you can see improvement beyond the scales.
  • Encouragement on tough days, and honest feedback when something needs to change.

Consistency over months is what drives results, and accountability is often the difference between starting and sticking with it. For a balanced look at the value, see is a personal trainer worth it.

5. General lifestyle and nutrition education

Training doesn't happen in isolation. Sleep, stress, daily movement and what you eat all affect your progress. A trainer can share general education on these areas – for example, healthy-eating basics in line with the Australian Dietary Guidelines, recipe ideas, and simple habits that fit your routine.

There are limits, though. Under the Cert IV in Fitness, individualised diet plans, specific diets and supplement advice sit outside a PT's scope (see unit SISFFIT026, training.gov.au). More detail is in can personal trainers give nutrition advice.

6. Referral to other professionals

A good trainer knows what they don't do. If something comes up that's outside their scope, they'll refer you on – for example to:

  • A GP for medical questions or before starting if you have a health condition.
  • A physiotherapist for pain or injury assessment and rehab.
  • An Accredited Practising Dietitian for individualised nutrition advice or medical nutrition needs.

This isn't a weakness – it's a sign of a trainer who puts your wellbeing first.

If you have an injury, ongoing pain or a medical condition, check with your GP or physio before starting a new training program, and tell your trainer so they can work around it.

What a personal trainer doesn't do

To set clear expectations, a personal trainer generally won't:

  • Diagnose or treat injuries or medical conditions.
  • Write individualised meal plans or prescribe supplements (unless they hold additional relevant qualifications).
  • Guarantee a specific amount of weight loss or a set result by a certain date.
  • Do the work for you – the training between sessions is still up to you.

In-person vs online: does the job change?

The core job is the same – assessment, programming, technique, accountability and education – but the delivery changes. In person, coaching happens live in the gym. Online, it happens through an app, check-ins, messaging and video feedback, while you train on your own. Many people use a mix of both. Read more in online coaching vs personal training.

Quick answers

Is a personal trainer just someone who counts reps?

No. Most of a trainer's value is in assessment, programming, technique coaching and helping you stay consistent over time.

Do personal trainers help with diet?

They can give general healthy-eating education in line with the Australian Dietary Guidelines. Individualised meal plans and supplement advice are outside a standard PT's scope.

Can a personal trainer help with injuries?

A trainer can adapt your program around an injury, but diagnosis and rehab belong with a GP or physiotherapist. A good trainer will refer you on.

What's the difference between a personal trainer and an online coach?

The core job is similar. An online coach delivers your program, check-ins and form feedback remotely, while an in-person trainer coaches you live in the gym.
