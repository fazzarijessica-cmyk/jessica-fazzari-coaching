Can I claim personal training on private health insurance in Australia?

Jessica Fazzari Coaching · 7 November 2026 · 5 min read

Sometimes, but it's far from automatic. Some Australian private health funds offer rebates on personal training under certain extras policies, usually when it's for managing a diagnosed health condition, often with a referral, and only when the trainer meets the fund's provider requirements. The only way to know for sure is to check with your fund before you book.

How private health rebates for personal training work

In Australia, rebates for personal training – where they exist – come through extras (general treatment) cover, not hospital cover. They're often grouped under a category such as health management or lifestyle programs, rather than as a standard benefit like dental or physio.

Whether you can claim depends on a mix of factors:

  • Your fund. Not every fund offers this benefit.
  • Your level of extras cover. It may only be included in higher or specific tiers.
  • The reason for training. Many funds only pay where training helps manage a diagnosed condition, not for general fitness.
  • Referral requirements. Some funds require a referral or a program approved by a GP or other health professional.
  • The provider. The trainer must meet the fund's requirements.
  • Limits and waiting periods. Annual limits and waiting periods usually apply.

Provider requirements: what funds look for

Funds that do offer a rebate generally want the trainer to meet certain standards. According to AUSactive, some funds – for example Bupa and Medibank – require the provider to be AUSactive-recognised and hold at least a Certificate IV in Fitness.

Each fund sets its own rules, and those rules can change. Being a qualified trainer doesn't automatically mean a trainer is set up to offer rebates through your particular fund. If claiming matters to you, ask the trainer directly whether they're a recognised provider with your fund – and confirm with the fund itself.

For more on what qualifications and registration involve, see what qualifications a personal trainer needs in Australia.

Questions to ask your health fund

Before you book, call your fund or check your policy documents online and ask:

  1. Does my extras policy include any benefit for personal training or exercise programs?
  2. Which benefit category does it fall under?
  3. Do I need a diagnosed condition, a referral or a health professional's approval?
  4. What requirements does the trainer need to meet to be eligible?
  5. How much is the rebate, and what's my annual limit?
  6. Is there a waiting period before I can claim?
  7. Are group sessions, online coaching or gym memberships treated differently from 1:1 sessions?
  8. What do I need to submit to claim – receipts, a referral, a program?

Write down the answers, including the name of the person you spoke to and the date, in case questions come up later.

Don't assume a session is claimable because a friend's was, or because a trainer is qualified. Funds, policy levels and provider requirements differ. Always confirm with your own fund first.

Keeping records if you do claim

If your fund confirms you're eligible, staying organised makes claiming easier and avoids surprises:

  • Keep a copy of any referral or approved program your fund requires.
  • Ask your trainer for receipts that show the details your fund needs, such as the provider's name and the date of each session.
  • Track how much of your annual limit you've used, so you know when it runs out.
  • Check when your limits reset – it may be the calendar year or the financial year, depending on your fund.
  • Re-check your cover if you change policies or funds, as benefits and waiting periods may change.

If you have a health condition

If you're looking at personal training to help manage a health condition, speak with your GP first. They can advise whether exercise is suitable, what to be careful of and whether another professional – such as a physiotherapist or accredited exercise physiologist – is more appropriate. Your GP is a good place to start.

A personal trainer can still be part of the picture, especially once you've been cleared for exercise. A good trainer will work within their scope and refer you on where needed – see what a personal trainer actually does.

If you can't claim: keeping personal training affordable

Many people pay for personal training without any rebate. If cost is a concern, there are ways to make it more manageable:

  • Fewer in-person sessions. See a trainer once a week and follow your program on your own the rest of the time.
  • Online coaching. A personalised program with check-ins usually costs less per week than regular 1:1 sessions.
  • Session packs. According to Bark (January 2026), packs of 10 or more sessions are often around 10–20% cheaper per session.
  • A focused block. Use a period of coaching to learn the basics, then train more independently.

Quick answers

Is there a waiting period to claim personal training?

Many extras benefits have waiting periods and annual limits. Ask your fund how long you need to hold your policy before you can claim.

Which health funds cover personal training?

It varies by fund and policy. AUSactive notes that some funds, such as Bupa and Medibank, require providers to be AUSactive-recognised with at least a Cert IV in Fitness. Check directly with your fund.

Do I need a referral to claim personal training?

Often, yes. Many funds only pay for personal training that helps manage a diagnosed condition, and may require a referral or approved program.

Can I claim online coaching on my health insurance?

Funds may treat online coaching differently from in-person sessions. Ask your fund whether online programs are eligible under your policy.
