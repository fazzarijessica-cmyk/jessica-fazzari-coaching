How much exercise do I need per week in Australia?

Jessica Fazzari Coaching · 7 November 2026 · 5 min read

According to Australia's Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour Guidelines (Department of Health), adults aged 18–64 should do 150–300 minutes of moderate activity or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity each week, or a mix of both. They should also do muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days a week, and be active on most – preferably all – days.

The Australian physical activity guidelines for adults

Here's a plain-English summary of the adult guidelines (18–64 years):

  • Be active on most, preferably all, days. Doing any physical activity is better than doing none.
  • Moderate activity: 150–300 minutes (2.5–5 hours) per week, or
  • Vigorous activity: 75–150 minutes (1.25–2.5 hours) per week, or an equivalent combination of both.
  • Muscle strengthening: on at least two days each week.
  • Sit less: minimise and break up long periods of sitting.

If you're doing none at the moment, the guidelines encourage you to start with some activity and build up gradually.

What counts as moderate vs vigorous exercise?

Moderate activity

Moderate activity raises your heart rate and makes you breathe a little harder, but you can still hold a conversation. Examples include brisk walking, an easy bike ride, swimming at a relaxed pace, or active jobs around the house and garden.

Vigorous activity

Vigorous activity makes you breathe hard enough that talking is difficult. Think running, fast cycling, hard swimming, team sports, or high-intensity conditioning work.

A simple way to compare the two: one minute of vigorous activity counts for roughly two minutes of moderate activity, which is why the vigorous target is about half the moderate one.

What counts as muscle-strengthening activity?

Muscle-strengthening activities make your muscles work against resistance. That includes:

  • Lifting weights – free weights, machines or cables.
  • Bodyweight exercises such as push-ups, squats, lunges and chin-ups.
  • Resistance band exercises.
  • Pilates and calisthenics-style training.
  • Heavy physical tasks like carrying or lifting.

The guidelines ask for this on at least two days a week. This is the part many people miss – they walk or run regularly but never do any strength work. Strength training helps you keep and build muscle, and makes everyday tasks easier. If you're not sure where to start, see personal training for beginners.

Why sitting less matters too

The guidelines don't only talk about exercise. They also recommend minimising and breaking up long periods of sitting. That matters for anyone with a desk job, a long commute or evenings on the couch – even if they train regularly.

Simple ways to sit less:

  • Stand up and move for a few minutes every half hour or so.
  • Take phone calls standing or walking.
  • Walk to a colleague instead of sending a message.
  • Get off public transport a stop early, or park further away.
  • Use part of your lunch break for a short walk.

None of these replace your workouts, but they add up across the week and help you be active on most days.

What a week meeting the guidelines could look like

There's no single right way to hit the targets. Here are two simple examples.

Example 1: Moderate-focused week

DayActivity
MondayStrength session (45 min)
TuesdayBrisk walk (30 min)
WednesdayBrisk walk (30 min)
ThursdayStrength session (45 min)
FridayBrisk walk (30 min)
SaturdayLonger walk or bike ride (60 min)
SundayRest or a gentle walk

Example 2: Busy schedule

  • Two or three 45-minute strength sessions that include some harder conditioning work at the end.
  • A 20–30 minute walk on most other days, even split into shorter bouts.
  • Getting up and moving regularly during long workdays.

These are general examples, not personal prescriptions. Your ideal week depends on your goals, current fitness and schedule.

If you have a medical condition, an injury, are pregnant or haven't been active for a while, talk to your GP or physio before significantly increasing your exercise. Start gradually and build up.

Is more exercise always better?

The guidelines give a range rather than a single number, so the lower figure is a starting point rather than a ceiling. But more isn't automatically better if it means you can't recover, get injured or burn out after a few weeks.

For most people, the biggest wins come from:

  1. Doing something most days.
  2. Including strength training at least twice a week.
  3. Staying consistent over months, not just a few weeks.
  4. Building up gradually rather than going from nothing to everything.

Quick answers

How many minutes of exercise should an adult do each week in Australia?

Adults aged 18–64 should aim for 150–300 minutes of moderate activity or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity a week, or a combination, according to the Department of Health guidelines.

How many days a week should I do strength training?

The Australian guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days each week.

Does walking count as exercise?

Yes. Brisk walking is a common example of moderate activity and counts towards the weekly target.

Can I split exercise into shorter sessions?

Yes. The guidelines encourage being active on most days, and doing any activity is better than none, so shorter bouts still add up.
